# Product Discovery and Product Management Roles

Research date: 2026-07-25

## Roles

- **Product Manager (PM)**: A job title (not a Scrum accountability) generally responsible for product strategy, roadmap, and value/business-viability outcomes. In Cagan's empowered-team model, the PM owns value risk and business viability risk, working alongside a designer (usability risk) and a lead engineer (feasibility risk) as a "product trio." (https://www.svpg.com/four-big-risks/, https://www.svpg.com/empowered-product-teams/)
- **Product Owner (PO)**: A Scrum accountability, not a job title — exists "if and only if" an organization uses Scrum. The PO holds ultimate ownership/accountability for the product backlog and its order; many (but not necessarily all) product-management activities are performed by the PO inside a Scrum context. Scrum.org frames the distinction as "manages" (PM) vs. "owns" (PO). (https://www.scrum.org/resources/blog/whats-difference-between-product-manager-and-product-owner)
- **Product Discovery Coach**: [unsourced — no primary source located for this as a distinct, formally defined role/title during this research; the function (coaching teams on continuous-discovery habits, interview cadence, opportunity solution trees) is described by Teresa Torres as something a PM/design/eng trio does themselves, or that an internal/external coach can facilitate, but no named methodology source defines "Product Discovery Coach" as a bounded role.] (https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees/)
- **Business Analyst (BA)**: Inward-facing role focused on operational detail, process/systems mapping, and translating business needs into implementable requirements ("the how"), versus the PM's outward-facing, market/strategy-oriented "why." In practice, in the absence of a PM (or when there is a leadership/skill vacuum) the BA can absorb PM-like responsibilities, and the two roles are often described as complementary in theory but blurred in practice. (https://www.aha.io/blog/the-product-manager-vs-the-business-analyst, https://jeffgothelf.com/blog/whats-the-difference-between-a-business-analyst-and-product-manager/)
- **Product Marketing Manager (PMM)**: Owns go-to-market for a product — positioning, messaging, sales enablement/training, launch campaigns, customer acquisition cost and lifetime-value-oriented KPIs — as distinct from the PM's product-creation/roadmap/delivery KPIs (speed to market, features delivered, satisfaction). The two roles are described as complementary across the product lifecycle from strategy to market adoption. (https://www.productplan.com/learn/product-manager-vs-product-marketing-manager, https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/finding-a-job/product-manager-vs-product-marketing-manager)
- **Growth PM**: A specialization that owns a specific business metric (e.g., activation, conversion, retention, monetization, viral loops) rather than the full lifecycle of a product or feature area; contrasted with a "core" PM who might own a feature like "checkout." Reforge frames "Growth Work" as creating and capturing value by accelerating adoption/usage among the existing market. (https://blog.logrocket.com/product-management/what-is-a-growth-product-manager-job-description-skills/, https://www.growthtalent.org/guides/role/product-growth-manager)

## Responsibility boundaries

- **PM vs. PO**: The PO accountability exists only within Scrum; a non-Scrum org needs a PM but not necessarily a PO. In many companies the same person holds both, but the underlying distinction — "manages" vs. "owns" the backlog/accountability — persists even when titles are merged. (https://www.scrum.org/resources/blog/whats-difference-between-product-manager-and-product-owner)
- **PM vs. BA**: In theory the split is clean — PM is strategic/outward-facing (market, customers, roadmap), BA is inward-facing (process, systems, internal stakeholders) — but "in practice, things can get tricky, especially when you bring UX/product designers into the mix and especially if there has been a leadership or skill vacuum." Both roles use data and require cross-functional communication, which is why people frequently transition between the two. (https://productschool.com/blog/career-development/product-manager-vs-business-analyst, https://www.aha.io/blog/the-product-manager-vs-the-business-analyst)
- **PM vs. PMM**: A relatively hard boundary in mature orgs — PM builds the product, PMM takes it to market — but the interface point is launch: PMM depends on PM-supplied positioning input (target customer, differentiation) and PM depends on PMM for market feedback and sales-enablement content. (https://www.productplan.com/learn/product-manager-vs-product-marketing-manager)
- **PM vs. Growth PM**: Overlapping skillset, differing scope — Growth PM's mandate is metric-bounded (e.g., "activation") rather than product/feature-bounded, so a Growth PM and a core-feature PM can both touch the same surface (e.g., checkout) for different reasons (conversion metric vs. feature completeness). (https://www.growthtalent.org/guides/role/product-growth-manager)
- **Feature teams vs. empowered/product teams (Cagan)**: In a feature team, the PM executes a prioritized list of features and dates handed down by stakeholders/executives, and "the value and business viability are the responsibility of the stakeholder or executive that requested the feature," not the PM. In an empowered/product team, the PM (with design and engineering) is given problems to solve and is accountable for outcomes, holding value and viability risk directly. This is presented by Cagan as the central boundary distinguishing real product management from feature-team order-taking. (https://www.svpg.com/product-vs-feature-teams/)

## Artifacts produced

- **PRD (Product Requirements Document)**: Traditional detailed spec of what to build; criticized for going stale quickly in fast-moving teams and for accumulating unresolved comment threads, but still defended for complex/regulated products where a durable reference is valuable. Consumed by engineering/design as the build spec in orgs that still use it. (https://blog.logrocket.com/product-management/prd-alternatives-modern-product-teams/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_requirements_document)
- **Product brief**: [unsourced — referenced generically across PM literature as a lighter-weight alternative to a full PRD, but no primary source was fetched in this research defining a canonical "product brief" format.]
- **Opportunity Solution Tree (OST)**: Visual tree with an outcome at the root, opportunities (customer needs/pain points/desires) as the next layer, solutions being explored, and assumption tests at the leaves. Built and maintained by the "product trio" (PM, designer, engineer) from continuous weekly customer interviews. Consumed by the trio itself for day-to-day discovery decisions and, at varying levels of detail, by stakeholders/executives for strategic visibility; it also feeds the product roadmap by identifying which vetted solutions to prioritize for delivery. (https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees/)
- **PR/FAQ (Amazon)**: A press release (heading, subheading, summary, problem, solution, customer quotes) plus an FAQ (external questions on pricing/functionality/support; internal questions spanning finance, legal, ops, engineering). Authored solo by a PM in early drafts (a few hours for draft one), iterated through many rounds (often 10+ drafts, 5+ leadership reviews) with reviewers explicitly favoring "truth-seeking vs. selling." Consumed by executive reviewers as the go/no-go gating artifact — approval allocates resources/timeline as described in the FAQ; rejection sends the idea back for rework or deprioritization. (https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process/)
- **Shape Up pitch**: Contains problem, appetite (time budget, typically six weeks), solution (the shaped approach with breadboards/fat-marker sketches), rabbit holes (known risks), and "no-gos" (explicit out-of-scope). Authored by senior shapers; consumed by the betting table (small group of high-level decision-makers: e.g., CEO, CTO, senior programmer, product strategist) to decide whether to fund the bet for the next six-week cycle. (https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.2-chapter-08, https://basecamp.com/shapeup/0.3-chapter-01)
- **User story map**: Two-dimensional artifact (Jeff Patton) — a horizontal "backbone" of epics/themes representing the user's journey, with vertical prioritization of the stories under each step. Explicitly designed to replace the "flat backlog," which Patton calls "a bag of context-free mulch," by preserving user-journey context. Consumed by the delivery team for release planning and backlog structuring. (https://storiesonboard.com/blog/jeff-patton-user-story-mapping, https://www.agilevelocity.com/blog/story-mapping-101)
- **Jira/Linear backlog items ("requirements")**: Discrete, sized, testable backlog items meeting a team's Definition of Ready (independent, negotiable, valuable, estimable, small, testable — INVEST-like criteria); refined by the PO/development team before Sprint Planning. Consumed directly by the delivery/engineering team as the unit of work. (https://www.scrum.org/resources/blog/ready-or-not-demystifying-definition-ready-scrum)
- **Roadmap**: [unsourced in this pass beyond generic PM-literature references — treated in the sources fetched as the downstream consumer of vetted OST solutions rather than as an artifact independently defined by a primary source here.] (https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees/)
- **Prototype (dual-track agile)**: Cagan states the prototype itself "serves as the spec for Delivery" — i.e., in mature dual-track practice, the discovery-stage prototype (validated via Lean UX "fake it before we make it") is the artifact that bridges discovery and delivery, not a separate written spec. (https://www.svpg.com/dual-track-agile/)

## Handoff points

- **Discovery → Delivery, general pattern**: In dual-track agile, "the outputs of discovery were the inputs of delivery" — originally framed by Cagan and Jeff Patton as two tracks within one team, not two teams. Patton's framing: "two tracks, not two teams" — developers participate in discovery and designers participate in delivery so accountability for outcomes stays unified. (https://www.svpg.com/dual-track-agile/)
- **PR/FAQ → build**: The gate is the go/no-go decision after PR/FAQ review; once approved, resources and timeline are allocated per the FAQ's own commitments, effectively converting the PR/FAQ into the founding spec for the build phase. (https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process/)
- **Shape Up pitch → cycle**: Handoff occurs at the betting table; once a bet is placed, the pitch (problem/appetite/solution/rabbit-holes/no-gos) becomes the brief handed to an autonomous, integrated team that then owns scope decisions within the fixed six-week appetite. (https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.2-chapter-08)
- **Where handoffs break down**: Teresa Torres documents that when organizations split people into separate "discovery" and "delivery" teams — a common misreading of dual-track agile — each PM→designer→engineer handoff loses context, "like a game of telephone," producing distorted requirements and software that misses customer needs. Her fix is not a better handoff artifact but eliminating the handoff: the same team (product trio, and to some extent the whole delivery team) engages directly with customers, showing discovery work continuously (experience maps, interview snapshots, OSTs) inside existing rituals (standups, sprint planning, retros) rather than delivering discovery as a finished package. (https://www.producttalk.org/2021/11/discovery-handoffs/)
- **Agency/outsourced delivery as an extreme case**: Handoff dysfunction is described as most acute when engineering is outsourced to an agency/contractors with no discovery involvement — the discovery-only team (often just designers and PMs, no technical voice) misses feasibility considerations, and the receiving engineering team ends up executing decisions it wasn't part of, described as being treated like "mercenaries." (https://www.svpg.com/discovery-delivery/, https://www.producttalk.org/2021/11/discovery-handoffs/)

## Gates and decision criteria

- **Definition of Ready (DoR)**: Not part of the official Scrum Guide but a widely used Scrum-community gate; a backlog item is "ready" when it is immediately actionable, has discussed value, has been estimated by the development team, and has PO-specified tests (testability) — commonly summarized via independent/negotiable/valuable/estimable/small/testable criteria. Refinement/readiness is judged jointly by the PO and development team before Sprint Planning. (https://www.scrum.org/resources/blog/ready-or-not-demystifying-definition-ready-scrum)
- **Shape Up betting table**: Small group of senior decision-makers (e.g., CEO, CTO, senior programmer, product strategist) meet for roughly one to two hours per cycle and decide which pitches get funded based on: staff availability, business priorities, recent work patterns, whether the problem matters and has "the right appetite," and solution attractiveness/timing. A "circuit breaker" rule prevents extending a bet past its fixed six-week appetite — the project either ships or stops, it does not get an extension by default. This is a hard, dated go/no-go/kill gate rather than a scoring formula. (https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.2-chapter-08, https://basecamp.com/shapeup/0.3-chapter-01)
- **PR/FAQ review gate**: Executive-level review explicitly optimized for "truth-seeking vs. selling" — reviewers assess whether the customer/problem is clearly defined, whether the proposed solution meaningfully addresses it, and whether the market opportunity justifies investment; approval funds the idea, rejection sends it back to be reworked, expanded, or deprioritized against the backlog. Authority to kill sits with the executive review group, not the authoring PM. (https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process/)
- **Prioritization frameworks as informal gates**:
  - **RICE** = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) / Effort — a numerical score used typically by PMs to rank items within their own domain. (https://blog.logrocket.com — cited via search synthesis, no full-page fetch succeeded for a single canonical URL; treat formula as [unsourced-primary in this pass, cross-corroborated across multiple secondary sources found in search])
  - **WSJF (Weighted Shortest Job First)** = factors in business value, time criticality, risk reduction, and job size; associated with SAFe-style portfolio-level prioritization across products/teams rather than single-feature ranking.
  - **Kano model**: classifies features by customer-satisfaction impact (must-haves, performance, delighters) rather than scoring numeric priority; used to clarify *what* customers value, often combined with RICE/WSJF rather than replacing them.
  - Note: the specific comparison ("PMs use RICE within their domain; portfolio managers use WSJF across products") came from aggregated search-result summaries rather than a single fetched primary page — flagged as **[unsourced-primary]** pending direct fetch, though the RICE/WSJF/Kano definitions themselves are consistent with well-established public descriptions of these named frameworks.
- **Who has authority to kill an idea**: Per the sources gathered, kill authority sits with whoever holds the corresponding gate — the PR/FAQ executive review panel at Amazon, the betting-table stakeholders in Shape Up, and (implicitly) the product trio itself in continuous discovery, which uses assumption-test failures within the OST to prune branches of the tree rather than requiring executive sign-off. (https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process/, https://basecamp.com/shapeup/2.2-chapter-08, https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees/)

## Practice variation and open debates

- **PO vs. PM terminology fight**: Scrum.org's own position is that the PO is an accountability that only exists within Scrum, and organizations not using Scrum should use "Product Manager" instead — implying that treating "Product Owner" as a universal job title (common in industry job postings) is a category error relative to the Scrum framework's original intent. (https://www.scrum.org/resources/blog/whats-difference-between-product-manager-and-product-owner)
- **"PRDs are dead" debate**: One camp argues traditional PRDs are structurally mismatched to fast iteration — they "rarely keep pace with ongoing work" and become outdated almost immediately, generating comment-thread rabbit holes on minutiae. The opposing camp argues PRDs retain value for complex or regulated products, functioning as valuable reference documents "when something goes wrong" even if treated as optional reading day-to-day. A middle position proposes lighter/alternative artifacts (e.g., interactive prototypes referred to as "Product Requirement Prototypes") instead of static text documents. (https://blog.logrocket.com/product-management/prd-alternatives-modern-product-teams/, https://uservoice.com/blog/is-the-product-requirements-document-dead>, https://alisx.com/insights/the-prd-is-dead-long-live-prp/)
- **Feature teams vs. empowered teams (Cagan's central framing)**: Cagan frames this not as a minor stylistic choice but as the primary fault line separating genuine product management from order-taking; he argues feature teams strip PMs of accountability for value/viability and shift it to requesting executives, which he treats as a root cause of low-quality, low-morale product organizations. (https://www.svpg.com/product-vs-feature-teams/, https://www.svpg.com/empowered-product-teams/)
- **Dual-track "two tracks, not two teams" misreading**: A recurring practice failure documented by both SVPG and Product Talk is organizations literally splitting staff into a "discovery team" and a "delivery team," which both sources treat as a misapplication of dual-track agile that reintroduces the exact handoff/telephone-game problems dual-track was designed to avoid. (https://www.svpg.com/dual-track-agile/, https://www.producttalk.org/2021/11/discovery-handoffs/)
- **Discovery intensity by context (agency vs. startup vs. big tech)**: The clearest sourced datapoint here is that agency/outsourced-engineering setups represent the most extreme and dysfunctional end of the discovery/delivery split, since the discovery-only team typically lacks a technical voice and the delivery-only team (often a contracted agency) receives decisions second-hand. Beyond this specific claim, this research did not surface a primary source directly contrasting discovery rigor across agencies vs. startups vs. big tech as a structured comparison — flagged as **[unsourced]** for the finer-grained claim.
